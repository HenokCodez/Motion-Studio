But this freedom creates a problem for companies that have built their businesses around selling software.

So, how do open-source developers make money?

Because here's the thing: making software isn't free, even when downloading it is.

Developers still need time, hardware, and money to maintain their projects.

And successful open-source businesses have figured out how to make money without charging everyone for the basic software.

The first model is paid convenience.

Take an open-source project and let people use it for free. Then charge for hosting, cloud access, automatic updates, or the infrastructure needed to run it.

The second is enterprise features and support.

A regular user might be happy with the free version. But a company managing thousands of employees may need advanced security, access controls, compliance tools, and someone to call when something breaks.

The third is selling services.

Companies can build open-source software and then charge for installation, customization, consulting, and maintenance.

And some projects survive through sponsorships, donations, or funding from companies that depend on them.

So the software can be free while the business around it still makes money.

But here's where things could get interesting.

If AI keeps making software cheaper and easier to build, I think we'll see more pressure on traditional software companies.

Instead of paying for dozens of separate applications, people may increasingly build small tools that do exactly what they need.

And companies will have to compete on more than just access to features.

But I wouldn't expect paid software to disappear completely.

Professional support, reliability, collaboration, and specialized features still have value. And open source doesn't automatically mean better security or a better product.

There's also a difference between open-source software and AI models that merely let you download their weights. The license and what's actually available still matter.

The bigger change is where the money goes.

If software becomes easier to reproduce, the advantage may shift toward the hardware, computing power, and services needed to run it.

Companies could find more value in selling cloud computing, specialized chips, and devices than in charging for basic software features.

That doesn't mean hardware prices will automatically rise, or that every company will abandon subscriptions. But it does mean the old model becomes harder to defend when free alternatives keep improving.

So, I don't think every paid application is going away.
